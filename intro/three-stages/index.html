Chapter 2 · The Frame

The Three Stages

Invent, manipulate, measure — and which one of them can be exact

One distinction before the results, and it is the only thing in this book you have to keep in your head. Doing algebra is three separate acts. They run on different rules, only one of them can be exact, and nearly every mistake in the subject — including three of mine, later in this book — is one act's move performed during another.

Three things you do

You want to know how much paint the wall takes.

First you decide the wall is a rectangle — a decision, not a fact. You left out the light switch, the texture, the corner that isn't square, and nothing forced any of it. Then you multiply 3.2 by 2.4, and that part has rules: do it right and nothing is lost, do it wrong and it is simply wrong. Then you read off 7.68 and buy eight litres. Whatever that number knew about the wall, the tin does not.

You invented an expression, you manipulated it, and you measured it. Everybody does all three, every time, and the middle one is the only one anybody has ever called exact.

Only the middle can be clean

Manipulation loses nothing when the operations keep two promises. We ask them of the operations, not of the numbers — "total" and "reversible" are not properties a number has.

Totality. Every operation is defined on every value. Nothing is off-limits, no case throws up its hands. There are no holes.
Reversibility. No operation destroys information. Whatever you did, there is a way back.1

Ordinary arithmetic keeps neither promise, and we have learned to live with both gaps. Division refuses a zero denominator: a hole. And one operation shreds the page without anyone flagging it —

a = b ⟶ 0·a = 0·b ⟶ 0 = 0

Every step is legal, and the last line has forgotten everything; it is true whatever a and b were. The rule responsible is absorption — 0·a = 0, zero swallowing whatever it touches — and it is the one thing this book removes.

Both ends are yours

Invention and measurement are the same act facing opposite directions. Both decide what to keep. Neither is forced, neither is precise, and nothing here makes them so — that is not a gap in the theory, it is what the two ends are.

The honest form of the claim, then, and it is narrow: This book does not make mathematics exact. It makes the middle exact and the two ends visible. The price still gets paid — once, where you can watch it being charged, instead of a little at a time on every line. Later the two ends turn out to be one knob, and you can watch it turn: The Addition Dial.

The mistake, which is always the same mistake

Four lines, any of which looks fine on its own:

0¹ = 0 0·ω = 1 (ω is zero's reciprocal) 0^(0·ω) = 0¹ = 0 (substitute 0ω = 1 in the exponent) 0^(0·ω) = (0^ω)⁰ = 1 (split the exponent, then x⁰ = 1)

The last two read the same thing two ways and get 0 and 1, so 0 = 1.

The fourth line is the culprit, and with the stages named the reason is one sentence: x⁰ = 1 is a measurement. It reads a value out and throws the exponent away — and the exponent is what the third line was using. A reading taken in the middle of a manipulation, which is the one thing the middle does not allow. Name the stage and you see it on sight instead of after an afternoon. The full account, and the escape, is How This Goes Wrong.

The price of admission

One axiom goes, and nothing is added back in its place.

An axiom costs more than itself: it costs every caveat it forces downstream. Each time a theorem says "for all x, provided x isn't the troublesome one," that provided is a tax the whole landscape pays forever. By that accounting the absorbing zero is the most expensive object in mathematics — a fence on every theorem that divides by a variable, and every exact value sitting on it outsourced to a limit. It buys tidiness: one well-behaved zero, a clean field. Not worth the sprawl.

So we don't fence it. We remove the line that planted it, delete nothing else,2 and watch what had been held out of reach come back unbidden — the reciprocal of zero, the imaginary unit, an exact circle. Refusing to destroy information does not only forbid things. It forces new values into existence, because the result an operation used to refuse now has to go somewhere.

How to read the rest

Every chapter says at the top which stage it is working in. The parts are named for whichever stage is under the microscope, not for chunks of the book — you are always doing all three.
