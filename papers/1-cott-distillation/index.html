What Falls Out of an Invertible Zero

A total, reversible algebra, and the structure that emerges when you refuse to throw anything away.

DoozerDiffuser · sibarum.github.io/cott
Distilled from working notes and conversation. Version: exploratory.
For entertainment and exploration only. This is a research program in progress, not a finished result. Several load-bearing claims are conjectures I am actively working to establish, and I have tried to mark them as such rather than smuggle them in as settled. Read it as a map of a frontier, not a survey of conquered ground. Where I write "falls out" or "is forced," I mean within the framework as constructed — whether the framework itself is consistent is the open question the whole thing rests on.

Most of mathematics is built by adding. You start with something small and safe, notice an operation that sometimes fails, and enlarge the world until the operation always works. Want subtraction to always make sense? The naturals aren't enough — move to the integers. Want division to always work? Move to the rationals. Want roots and logarithms of negatives? Move to the complex numbers. Each step is the same move: widen the domain so a useful operation stops throwing exceptions.

There is one exception that everyone agrees to leave standing. Division by zero. It's treated as a different kind of failure — not "we haven't built the world big enough yet," but "this is genuinely forbidden, don't ask." And I want to take seriously the possibility that this last exception is exactly like all the others: a place where we stopped widening the world too early, and then built a century of machinery to route around the gap instead of closing it.

This document is about what happens if you refuse to stop. If you insist — really insist, with no compromises — that every operation be total (always defined) and every operation be reversible (never destroys information), and then you just follow the algebra wherever it goes. It turns out it goes somewhere. The imaginary unit shows up on its own. Complex rotation shows up on its own, without limits or power series. And a lot of the scaffolding we think of as load-bearing turns out to be optional — not wrong, just provincial, true only inside a particular projection we mistook for the whole.

01 The two demands, and the one thing they forbid

Start with the observation that "total" and "reversible" are not properties of numbers. They're properties of operations. So the honest thing is to stop caring what counts as a number at all, and instead define a type by the operations it's closed under without loss. That's the whole starting move:

Definition A type is characterized not by intrinsic properties of its elements, but by the total, reversible operations it admits and remains stable under. Numbers are secondary — they're representations of what the operations produce. A value that can't yet be written as a single number isn't undefined; it's just an operational history that hasn't collapsed to a numeral.

Totality is the easy demand to state: when an operation would normally be undefined, we don't forbid it — we carry the unresolved structure forward explicitly, deferring the collapse instead of crashing. Reversibility is subtler, and it's worth being careful. It does not mean every operation is a bijection. It means no operation performs an irreversible collapse — there's always a way back, even if the way back takes several steps or some auxiliary bookkeeping.

Now here's the thing classical algebra never says out loud. We all know division by zero is the problem child. But multiplication by zero is just as guilty, and it's guilty of the exact crime reversibility forbids:

multiply both sides by 0: a = b ⟶ 0·a = 0·b ⟶ 0 = 0

That step is "legal" — you did the same thing to both sides. And it vaporized everything you knew. In the usual telling, division fails to be total and we make a fuss about it; but multiplication fails to be reversible and we say nothing, because we've decided that particular information-destruction is fine. Under the two demands, it is not fine. It's the same defect, and it has to go.

Division isn't total. Multiplication isn't reversible. Both are the same wound, and the whole theory is what happens when you refuse to leave either one open.

02 Zero stops absorbing

The reason 0·a = 0 destroys information is a single axiom: absorption. Zero eats whatever it touches. That's the property we have to give up — carefully, because we don't want to give up anything else. And the careful version is deeper than "make zero behave." It is that there was never a primitive zero to misbehave in the first place.

Cancellation is not an element you land on — it is an operation. Write it ⊘: "these two terms annihilated, erase both." Solid What survives an annihilation is not ⊘ but a residue, and which residue survives depends on the association the cancellation was caught in:

x − x = ⊘ → *0 subtractive cancel — deposits a 0 if bound in a product x / x = ⊘ → +1 divisive cancel — deposits a 1 if bound in a sum

Read them operationally. When a term subtracts from itself, erase both — and if that pair stood in direct multiplicative association with some other term, a 0 is left in its place. When a term divides itself, erase both — and if that pair stood in direct additive association, a 1 is left. In each cancellation's home association it leaves nothing at all: z + (x−x) = z and z·(x/x) = z, clean. Only in the foreign association is a residue deposited. So 0 and 1 are not primitives — they are the two shadows one operation casts when it fires in the wrong kind of company: 0 is a subtractive cancel seen multiplicatively, 1 a divisive cancel seen additively. The additive ↔ multiplicative duality the whole theory runs on is already present in the two ground grains.

No absorbing identity is baked into the ground floor. There is one operation, ⊘, and two residues — and the residues are exactly the classical 0 and 1, caught in the act of being made.

Why leave a residue at all — why not erase to nothing everywhere? Reversibility forces it. Solid A self-cancel may be dropped into a sum for free: y = z is identical to y = z + (x−x), since adding nothing changes nothing. It may not be dropped into a product: y = z never licenses y = z·(x−x), which would annihilate y. Now run simplification backward. A +(x−x) can reverse to nothing, because it could have entered by the free insertion. But a (x−x) sitting as a factor could not have arrived legally — it must have come from a pre-existing 0 factor — so the reverse step is forced to restore that 0. The residue is the memory that a cancellation happened inside a product. Erase it to bare nothing and y = 0z silently collapses to y = z; the calculation stops being reversible. The 0 is not debris — it is the receipt that keeps the ledger balanced, and it is the same receipt discipline the rest of this program lives by.

This is exactly why the classical picture felt compelled to make 0 absorb. It had fused three separate jobs onto one glyph — additive identity, annihilator, "no quantity" — and absorption is what you get when the annihilator and the identity are forced to be the same object. Traction splits them. ⊘ takes annihilation, and because it is an operation, not an element, it never needs a reciprocal: the field theorem that forbids inverting the identity has nothing to bite on. That frees 0 to be an ordinary, invertible, structured element — the invertible zero this whole document is about was never the thing that absorbs. The residue, once deposited and bound to a variable, is simply tagged and carried, never freely cancelled — which is the invariance condition for discharge stated as bookkeeping:

0·x := 0x (a bound zero-displacement; may not be freely cancelled)

And when zero binds to a whole expression, something nice happens — it lifts into a structural differential, with no limits anywhere in sight:

0·f(x) := f(x + 0x) − f(x)

Watch this do actual work on f(x) = x² + c:

0·x² = (x + 0x)² + c − x² − c = 2x(0x) + (0x)² divide by the displacement (legal — 0x is structured, not scalar zero): (0·x²)/(0x) = 2x + 0x ≈ 2x

There's the derivative. No epsilon, no delta, no "let dx approach zero." The higher-order term (0x)² was never discarded — it's sitting right there as a visible residue, and the ≈ at the end means "select the linear component," not "destroy the rest." This is close in spirit to dual numbers, but dual numbers impose nilpotency (ε² = 0) as an axiom. Here nothing is imposed. The structure is retained and reversibility holds all the way through. That difference — retain versus discard — is the whole personality of the theory in miniature.

On the notation "=" vs "≈" Throughout: = means an information-conserving rewrite (you can always run it backward). ≈ means a contextual identification that does lose something — a projection, a truncation. Keeping these two apart, visibly, is not pedantry. It's the honesty that the whole framework is built to enforce: every place information leaves, you can see it leave.

03 A reciprocal for zero

Once zero no longer absorbs, the prohibition on dividing by it loses its foundation. (This is not a coincidence — the impossibility of 1/0 in a field is a theorem, and its proof uses absorption. Remove absorption and you've removed the proof.) So we give zero a reciprocal and a name:

1/0 := ω

The one thing ω has to satisfy, to make cancellation total rather than conditional, is the discharge condition:

0·ω = 1

And that's the core axiom of the entire structure. Everything downstream is bookkeeping around it. A few immediate consequences, none of them assertions about classical arithmetic — just structural facts about a system where zero participates symmetrically instead of exceptionally:

x/0 = ωx x/x = 1 (no exceptions) 0/0 = 1

That last one deserves a note, because it's the kind of thing that makes people reach for the fire alarm. 0/0 = 1 is not a claim that "zero over zero is one" in the sense you were taught it's undefined. It's the statement that in this system, x/x = 1 is allowed to hold without carving out an exception for x = 0. Wheel theory — the closest existing relative — also gives zero a reciprocal, but it pays by introducing a "bottom" element ⊥ for 0/0, a genuine terminal collapse. We don't want the collapse. We normalize 0/0 to 1 and keep moving. Different choice, different theory.

Notice what we did and didn't do. We did not add axioms. We removed one — absorption — and let the reciprocal fall into the hole it left. That subtractive move is the aesthetic of the whole program: strip a restriction without losing structure. It's the least glamorous exercise in mathematics and, I'd argue, one of the most underrated.

04 Exponentiation forces the imaginary unit

Here's where it stops being bookkeeping and starts being surprising. We extend exponentiation to the boundary elements 0 and ω using only identities that already hold for every other base. No new axioms — just "apply the rules you already believe, uniformly, and don't flinch at the boundary."

x⁰ = 1 ⟹ 0⁰ = 1 and ω⁰ = 1 x¹ = x ⟹ 0¹ = 0 and ω¹ = ω x⁻¹ = 1/x ⟹ 0⁻¹ = ω and ω⁻¹ = 0

Every integer power is now determined. Only two values are left unfixed by the standard rules: 0^ω and ω^ω. Requiring the image to close reversibly pins both. And the value that closure forces is the one worth the price of admission:

0^ω = −1

Take a breath at that. If you know Euler's identity, e^(iπ) = −1, you should feel a chime. Because look what happens when we chase it. Base-zero logarithm is defined as the inverse of base-zero exponentiation, and it obeys the ordinary quotient rule. So:

log₀(−1) = log₀(1/0) = log₀(1) − log₀(0) = 0 − 1 = ω

Confirmed from the other direction: 0^ω = −1. And now the imaginary unit is not far — it's just a square root of that half-turn. Write any negative base through base-zero exponentiation and its inverse:

(−1)^(1/2) = 0^((1/2)·log₀(−1)) = 0^(ω/2) so: i = 0^(ω/2)

And it behaves. The powers of 0^(ω/2) cycle exactly the way powers of i cycle:

(0^(ω/2))⁰ = 1 (0^(ω/2))¹ = 0^(ω/2) = i (0^(ω/2))² = 0^ω = −1 (0^(ω/2))³ = −0^(ω/2) = −i (0^(ω/2))⁴ = 1

The imaginary unit was never adjoined. It was hiding inside an invertible zero the whole time, waiting for exponentiation to read it out.

The significance is a matter of accounting. The standard construction of ℂ imports three heavy objects: you posit i as a new element, you define exp through an infinite series and its convergence, and you define π through a limit. Here, i is derived, the rotation is algebraic, and no limit appears. That doesn't make the machinery free — the cost relocates into requiring that zero be oriented and graded, which is its own structure to justify — but one primitive generating three derived objects is a better trade than three posited objects. Whether it's a legitimate trade is exactly the projection question of §8.

05 Chebyshev, or: rotation without analysis

If i = 0^(ω/2) is real structure and not a coincidence, it should survive being pushed on. So push on it: tabulate the powers of 0 at half-integer steps and see whether they hold together or fall apart. They hold together — and the thing they hold together into is a structure Chebyshev found in the 19th century for entirely unrelated reasons.

Introduce the half-step generators and their sum:

u = 0^(1/2) v = ω^(1/2) = 0^(−1/2) u·v = 0⁰ = 1 Chebyshev parameter: s = u + v half-cycle sum: aₙ = uⁿ + vⁿ = 0^(n/2) + ω^(n/2)

These sums satisfy a recurrence — and it's the modified Chebyshev recurrence of the first kind, exactly:

aₙ = s·aₙ₋₁ − aₙ₋₂ a₀ = 2, a₁ = s
naₙ = uⁿ + vⁿ

These are exact symbolic identities, not approximations. Every one is a polynomial in s with integer coefficients. And the critical observation — the one that turned this from a curiosity into something I trust — is that they refuse to collapse. There are a dozen opportunities for contradiction in here: zero is invertible, powers go negative, exponents go fractional. Any of those could have produced 1 = 0 and ended the story. None of them do. The recurrence holds cleanly at every step.

Since u + v = s and u·v = 1, the generator u is a root of its minimal polynomial over ℚ[s]:

u² − s·u + 1 = 0 ⟹ u² = s·u − 1 (reduction rule)

which makes every element of the ring ℚ[s][u]/(u² − su + 1) reducible to a canonical form a + b·u with a, b ∈ ℚ[s]. Exact rational arithmetic, closed, computable, with a conjugation that swaps u ↔ v (the zero-class and omega-class generators) and a norm under which every power of the generator is a unit of norm 1. And it doesn't stop at one level. Adjoin the quarter-power 0^(1/4), then 0^(1/8), and you get a dyadic tower of quadratic extensions, dimensions doubling 2, 4, 8, 16, …, each level linked to the last by the Chebyshev doubling formula tₖ² = tₖ₋₁ + 2.

Why this is the strongest evidence so far I did not design the Chebyshev structure. I demanded totality and reversibility, tabulated powers of an invertible zero, and a well-known family of orthogonal polynomials fell out — one discovered independently, for unrelated reasons, over a century ago. When a constraint you imposed for aesthetic reasons reproduces something nature already found by another road, that's the kind of coincidence that's usually not a coincidence. The whole phase structure of the complex numbers, reconstructed with exact rational arithmetic, no power series, no branch cuts, no real-number completion underneath. That is the result I'd stake the program on.

06 The reframe — zero as a unit seen edge-on

Here's the shift that made the rest click for me, and it's less a theorem than a change of eye. We're trained to think of zero as small — the additive identity, the absence of magnitude, the origin where nothing is. That's the picture the real number line hands us, and the real number line is a very particular, very flat slice of things.

Try the opposite. Suppose zero has unit magnitude — that it's a full-sized unit vector — and that what we call "0" is only its shadow on the real axis. A unit vector pointing perpendicular to the real line casts a shadow of length zero. Not because it's small. Because it's pointing in a direction the real line can't see.

Under that reading, "treat zero as a unit and it becomes i" stops being mystical. Of course it does — a unit orthogonal to the reals, with the projection stripped off, is the imaginary unit. It explains why the signed zero −0 is a genuinely distinct object with the same shadow: +0 and −0 are +i and −i seen edge-on, two different units projecting to the same point. It explains why ω = 1/0 lands where it does — the reciprocal of a unit is a unit, and 1/i = −i, so ω and −0 are close relatives rather than opposite ends of anything.

And it says the real line was lying to us about distance. On the projective line, 0 and ∞ sit at opposite poles, maximally far apart. But that separation is an artifact of the projection — it took a single oriented unit and its sign-flip and stretched the sign into the whole diameter of the sphere. Seen in the algebra rather than the shadow, the two poles are a sign apart, not a sphere apart.

Then the last clause, the one that keeps the door open: zero is the shadow of a unit — but which unit, and from what space? Project a unit from ℂ and zero unfolds into ±i. Project from the quaternions and it unfolds into a whole sphere of imaginary units. From higher algebras, a bigger sphere still. Zero is the universal real-shadow of "an imaginary unit of whatever algebra you're standing in," and which hypercomplex unit it lifts to is precisely the choice of projection. That's what "something beyond i, depending on what's being projected" means. The origin isn't the emptiest point on the line. It's the whole orthogonal unit-sphere of the ambient algebra, seen end-on.

07 Why the tower can't close (and why that's the good news)

Every classical algebra you know is bought by imposing a restriction that destroys information. A field imposes absorption (zero eats everything — magnitude collapses). Commutative multiplication collapses the two division-orders into one. Associativity collapses bracketing history. Norm-multiplicativity — the property behind Hurwitz's theorem, which caps the "nice" division algebras at exactly four (ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆) — collapses direction-under-product down to a scalar. Each of these is a one-way door. Each one, once you walk through it, you can't walk back.

The information-conservative structure is the one that declines to walk through any of them. And the moment you decline them, the ceiling comes off. The Cayley–Dickson construction that builds ℝ → ℂ → ℍ → 𝕆 doesn't actually stop at the octonions — it keeps doubling forever, into the sedenions and beyond. Everyone treats the step past 𝕆 as a disaster, because that's where zero divisors appear: nonzero things whose product is a shadow. But zero divisors are exactly oriented-zeros-with-orientation. In this framework they aren't a pathology to be outlawed; they're the native content the whole thing is built on. Hurwitz's "there are only four" really means "there are only four if you insist on throwing away magnitude-direction under multiplication" — and we don't insist. So there are infinitely many, and the classical four are just the shadows that survived the insisting.

The conjecture the openness rests on I believe absorption is the most basic of the one-way projections, and that a structure refusing all of them sits upstream of every classical algebra and can project down to any of them. Sharpened honestly: the collapsing invariants form a lattice, and the full information-conservative traction is its single top element — the join of everything that hasn't yet collapsed. That is a real, falsifiable claim, and the way it could fail is specific: if two of the invariants turn out to be jointly unrefusable — impossible to decline at the same time without contradiction — then there's no top element and no universal structure. So the work isn't to show non-absorption is universal. It's to show the invariants can all be declined at once, consistently. I'm well on the way, but I have not finished it, and until I have, the open generator is a hypothesis, not a theorem.

If the conjecture holds, then something quietly enormous follows, and it's the part I care about most. In an entropic universe, complexity comes from things breaking — symmetries shatter, information leaks, and structure precipitates out of the wreckage. But a genuinely reversible, information-conserving substrate can't make complexity that way, because nothing is ever lost. Its complexity has to come from elaboration — the ground element unfolding structure it already contained. And "refuse to destroy information" is provably unbounded: the tower can't close. A conserving universe that stayed simple is not on the menu. Demand totality and reversibility and unbounded structure is entailed, not permitted.

If complexity didn't emerge from nothing, we wouldn't be here to notice. The refusal to throw anything away is not a stylistic preference. It may be the reason there's something rather than nothing.

And "nothing," in this telling, was never nothing. It was everything, seen edge-on.

08 What's actually unproven

I want to end with the debts, plainly, because a theory that hides its debts is doing the exact thing this one was built to stop doing. Here is what is not settled, in order of how much weight it carries.

Consistency (joint-refusability). The whole edifice assumes the invariants — absorption, commutativity, associativity, norm-multiplicativity — can all be declined together without deriving a contradiction. An inconsistent system feels wonderful right up until someone proves 1 = 0 inside it, at which point it proves everything and means nothing. This is the ballgame. Everything below rests on it.

The projection theorem. The symbol 0 — used deliberately, refusing to rename it to something safer — is a promissory note. It claims that the classical zero is my zero projected: that standard absorptive arithmetic is this structure modulo the imposition of absorption, with the classical axioms provably recovered. Until that recovery is proven, "the field's exception for zero is a projection artifact" is a conjecture wearing a symbol rather than a theorem forcing one. I keep the symbol because renaming it would concede the very point at issue — that the restricted zero and the free zero are two different things, when the claim is that they're one thing at two grades. But I owe the theorem, and I know I owe it.

The carrier and the transcendentals. "Calculus without limits" is real for the algebraic and rational part — the structural differential of §2 needs no limits, and the Chebyshev tower gives exact phase with no completion underneath. But limits historically do two jobs: they compute derivatives (dispensable here) and they build the objects that need completing — the real continuum, the transcendental functions. I owe a clean account of where sin, exp, and the continuum live in this framework, and whether "limits dissolve" means "from the same objects" or the more modest "I never built the objects that needed them." Those are different wins and I don't want to conflate them.

The wild part of the tower. Past the octonions, the zero divisors aren't a tidy set — they form a genuine variety with real geometric structure. Whether the information-conservative traction can carry that variety as legitimate graded content, or whether it needs a canonical normal form to tame it into something computable, is open. The running implementation (exact rational numerator/denominator with sign, 0/0 normalized to 1/1) lives in a tame projection where this problem doesn't bite — which means the implementation validates the accessible core and is silent on exactly the part where the hardest claims live.

So: the ideas are, I think, better than the proofs behind them right now. That gap is the entire content of the next stretch of work — and it's a gap I'd rather state out loud than paper over, because the honest ledger is the only one worth keeping. If the two central theorems come through, this stops being a promising outsider's framework and becomes a thing that has to be reckoned with. If they can't come through — if the invariants won't jointly refuse — I want to find that out as fast as possible, because that's information too, and information is the one thing this theory was built never to waste.

Constructive Operational Type Theory comprises three interlocking parts: Principled Emergence Metatheory (an attempt to make "elegance" a measurable design criterion — fewer axioms, justified from core principles, reusing existing structure by reframing), Traction Theory (the total, reversible, exception-free algebra sketched here), and Mirror Calculus (where 0 reads as differentiation, ω as integration, and 0ω = 1 as their composition).
