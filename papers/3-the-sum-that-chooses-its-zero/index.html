The Sum That Chooses Its Own Zero

Multiplication in a world with an invertible zero was settled long ago. Addition was the wound left open — so I tried to grow one from 0 and ω, and watched which construction survived its own consequences.

Traction hands you a beautiful multiplication for free. A value carries a coefficient and a grade; you multiply by multiplying the coefficients and adding the grades; 0 sits at grade +1, ω at grade −1, and 0·ω = 1 is just the two grades cancelling to the unit. Reciprocal flips the grade. Everything is total, everything is reversible, and — as the first document showed — the imaginary unit falls out of the boundary without a single limit. Multiplication is a solved problem.

Addition is not. And it's not solved for a reason worth stating precisely, because the reason is the whole plot: the graded carrier resists ordinary addition at exactly the place multiplication found easiest. This document is the record of pushing on that resistance until it gave — trying to build a sum out of 0 and ω the way the first document built i, discarding the constructions that tore, and keeping the two that didn't. The survivor is not a new axiom. It's a dial, and the dial already knew about the pole.

01 The wound multiplication left open

Here is the asymmetry, and it is exact rather than vibes. Multiplication's only indeterminate form is 0·ω, and it is cured by a single integer: grades add, +1 and −1 cancel, done. That is the entire reason the graded carrier works — it took the one bad product and unrolled it into an integer that bookkeeps itself. Multiplication's wound is one-dimensional, and one dimension of grade closes it.

Addition's wound is not one-dimensional. Solid Watch a cancellation of leading terms — two graded values whose tops are a and −a. The tops annihilate cleanly (a + (−a) = ⊘, a subtractive cancel in its additive home, no residue). But in a graded world you need to know the grade of what is left, and a leading-term cancellation is precisely the event that refuses to tell you. The top vanished; what remains lives at some higher grade you cannot see without carrying the entire tail. Multiplication resolves its indeterminacy with one integer; addition would need the whole series. That is the difference between a valuation and a germ, and it is why "just track the grade" — which was the making of multiplication — is not enough for addition.

So the honest starting position is: the algebra is a multiplicative success and an additive vacancy. You cannot import ordinary +, because ordinary + assumes an ungraded field and the whole point is that we left it. The question is whether an addition can be grown that respects the grading instead of fighting it.

02 First attempt: make the grade carry the sum

The obvious first move — and the one I chased longest — is to make the grade do for addition what it did for multiplication. Push the identity −0 = ω, let the grade absorb the difficulty, and hope cancellation lands somewhere nameable. It's the right instinct and it fails in an instructive way, so it earns its section.

The failure has a precise location: there are two different inverses trying to wear one minus sign. Solid Reciprocal flips the grade — recip(0) = ω, grade +1 ↦ −1 — and it's the multiplicative inverse, the thing satisfying 0·ω = 1. Negation keeps the grade and flips the coefficient's sign, and it's the additive inverse. The seductive equation −0 = ω is true only if the − means reciprocal. Read as negation it's false: neg(0) is a different grade-+1 object, not ω at all. Fold the two inverses together and you get an addition that looks consistent for a page and then quietly derives a grade it can't defend.

Why this was worth failing at Solid The tear tells you the fix. Ordinary addition jams because its identity — 0 — sits at the grade that swallows information, the grade where cancellation dumps its unknowable remainder. If the identity of your addition is the same object that eats grades, cancellation has nowhere legal to land. So the repair isn't to patch the grade arithmetic. It's to move the additive identity off the swallowing grade entirely. Which points, unavoidably, at the other pole.

03 The move that holds: add through the reciprocal

If the additive identity must not be 0, make it ω. The clean way to do that isn't to legislate it — it's to conjugate ordinary addition by the one map that swaps the two poles. Add by reciprocal:

a (+) b := 1/(1/a + 1/b) = a*b/(a+b)

This is harmonic addition — the sum that resistors in parallel obey, that thin lenses obey, that reduced mass obeys. And every one of the boundary facts we wanted falls straight out of it, none of them posited: Solid

ω ⊕ x = x (ω is the additive identity) 0 ⊕ x = 0 (0 absorbs — it is the new "point at infinity" of ⊕) x ⊕ neg(x) = ω (cancellation lands on ω, a definite pole)

Look at that third line, because it is the whole reason this construction survives where §2 died. In ordinary addition, x + (−x) targets 0 — the grade-swallowing pole, the one whose order you can't recover. In harmonic addition, x ⊕ neg(x) = −x²/0 = ω — a definite element. The reciprocal took the one event that destroyed grade information and pointed it at the pole that can hold it. Cancellation stopped being a loss and became a receipt, filed at ω.

Ordinary addition sends cancellation into the dark. Reciprocal addition sends it to the pole — and the pole, in this algebra, is a place you're allowed to stand.

04 What that addition is, exactly

No hand-waving here — this one is nailed down. Conjugate the whole structure by φ(x) = 1/x and it unmasks completely: Solid

(everything, ⊕, ×) ≅ (ℝ, +, ×) via x ↦ 1/x ω ↔ 0 (the additive zero) 1 ↔ 1 (the unit, fixed) 0 ↔ ∞ (the excluded pole)

It is the real field wearing a reciprocal coordinate, with the roles of 0 and ∞ exchanged. Total, associative, commutative — and, the fact that makes it a field rather than two operations sharing a carrier, multiplication distributes over it:

a·(x ⊕ y) = (a·x) ⊕ (a·y) both sides = a·xy/(x+y)

Two things this is not, said plainly because the temptation to overclaim is real. It is not the Riemann sphere: the sphere is a quotient that forgets coefficient and grade, keeping only the ratio, which is exactly why it has no group structure and no total addition; this retains coefficient and grade and is a clean field precisely because it declined to quotient. And it does not extend ordinary addition — it replaces it. 2 ⊕ 3 = 6/5, not 5. Plausible that this is the addition traction actually wants; Solid that it is an addition, exact and closed. Whether it's the canonical one is a question §5 refuses to let me answer too quickly.

05 A second, truer sum: the phase chart

Because there is a second survivor, and it has a stronger claim to being the addition — it's the one already latent in the first document. Recall i = 0^(ω/2), and the reframe's quiet consequence that 0 ↔ i and ω ↔ 1/i = −i. So put the carrier where that correspondence lives: on the circle, a value written e^(iπa), with the grade a now a real-valued phase. Then ordinary complex addition of two such points is not a mess — it's a clean trigonometric identity: Solid

e^(iπa) + e^(iπb) = 2·cos(π(a−b)/2) · e^(iπ(a+b)/2)

Read the two factors and the duality with multiplication leaps out. The phase of the sum is (a+b)/2 — the average of the two grades. The magnitude is 2cos of half the grade-gap. So:

operationwhat it does to the grade
×  (multiply)grades add — e^(iπa)·e^(iπb) = e^(iπ(a+b))
+  (add)grades average — mean (a+b)/2, weighted by 2cos

This is a genuinely total, genuinely associative addition — trivially, because in these coordinates the carrier is ℂ and the operation is ordinary complex +. It is the resolution of the thing the second document kept circling: you cannot make the Riemann sphere add (no compact surface of the wrong Euler characteristic can be a group), but you can make the plane add, and the phase chart is the plane. The cost is honest and specific: in this chart 0 and ω demote to the ordinary points i and −i, the true additive identity becomes the origin (a genuinely new point, i + (−i) = 0, which the identity above returns as 2cos(π/2) = 0), and the grade must run continuously rather than by integer steps. Plausible that this — not §4 — is the "real" traction addition, precisely because it's the one that honors i = 0^(ω/2) instead of routing around it.

So I had two survivors and a decision I couldn't make on taste alone. That's usually the sign that you're holding two views of one object and haven't found the axis between them yet. There was an axis — two of them, as it turned out, and the first one runs straight through the cos I had just written down without looking at it hard enough.

06 One identity, three regimes — the cos was never the point

I read the cos in §5 as content: the phase chart's own contribution, the specific shape circular geometry imposes on a sum. It isn't. That identity uses no property of i whatsoever. It is pure factoring: Solid

e^x + e^y = e^((x+y)/2) · ( e^((x−y)/2) + e^(−(x−y)/2) ) = 2·cosh((x−y)/2) · e^((x+y)/2)

Split the exponent into its mean and its half-difference, pull the mean out front, and what remains is even in the half-difference. That is the entire derivation, and it holds in any commutative algebra where exp converges — no sign, no dimension, no choice of generator enters anywhere. §5 is this identity at x = iπa, together with cosh(iu) = cos u. So mean-plus-correction is not a discovery about the phase chart. The phase chart only chooses which correction.

Then what does the choosing? A single scalar, and this is the part worth having. cosh is even, so only even powers appear, so it factors through squaring: Solid

cosh x = Σ x^(2n)/(2n)! = f(x²), f(t) := Σ tⁿ/(2n)!

where f is a perfectly ordinary entire function of one variable. Read that line slowly, because it carries the section: cosh cannot see x. It can only see x². The moment x² is a scalar, the transcendental evaluates on a number and returns a number — and the whole zoo of cases collapses into f applied to three signs.

d²correction f(d²)algebra ℝ[d]orbit of expwhat the sum does
−1cosℂ = Cl(0,1)circleaverage, correction can vanish
01dual numbers = Cl(0,0,1)linepure average, no correction
+1coshsplit-complex = Cl(1,0)hyperbola branchaverage, correction ≥ 1 always

The middle row is the one to notice, and it is the same phrase I have been using since §5 without realising it had a home: pure average. At d² = 0 the correction is identically 1, and e^x + e^y = 2e^((x+y)/2) exactly. Checkable by hand — (1+εa) + (1+εb) = 2(1 + ε(a+b)/2). Averaging the grade with no residue is not a special feature of the phase chart; it is what a degenerate square does.

And "square is a scalar" has a name. Solid It is the defining relation of a Clifford algebra, v² = Q(v). My three rows are precisely the Clifford algebras of the three rank-one quadratic forms, signature −1, 0, +1. This is worth saying carefully, because the tempting reading is wrong: Clifford algebra is not an alternative framing of what I found, and it is not a generalization I could adopt or decline. It is the classification of it. The condition that makes the closed form exist at all — one scalar for f to eat — is the condition Clifford is built from.

Two boundaries on that, so it doesn't get oversold. cosh does not need a scalar square: the series converges in any Banach algebra, so cosh of an arbitrary matrix or operator exists. Clifford is what makes it closed-form instead of requiring diagonalization. And inside a Clifford algebra the closed form covers only elements whose square is scalar — vectors always, some bivectors (in Cl(3,0), B² = −|B|², which is exactly why rotors work), a general multivector never.

A retraction, because I had the interesting case backwards Solid My first instinct was that the hyperbolic row was the rich one — a real cosh of a real argument, a genuine hyperbola, zero divisors on the null cone. That reading is empty and I want it on the record as retracted. Split-complex numbers diagonalize: e± = (1±j)/2 are orthogonal idempotents, ℝ[j] ≅ ℝ ⊕ ℝ, and e^(jθ) = e^θ e₊ + e^(−θ) e₋. So the hyperbolic row is the real identity run twice, once per null coordinate, with the same amplitude factor in both slots because cosh is even — which is precisely why it collects into a scalar out front. Nothing new happens there. Worse, I had noted that two hyperbolic exponentials can't cancel "despite the zero divisors," which under diagonalization reduces to: two positive reals don't sum to zero. That is positivity of exp, dressed up as a fact about the null cone. The elliptic row is the outlier, and §7 says why on a ground that survives diagonalization.

07 What separates them is compactness, not cos versus cosh

There is a real temptation here, and I fell into it for a full page: to treat cos ↔ cosh as a substitution that leaves the algebra untouched. Almost true, and the exact size of the "almost" is informative.

Every classical trigonometric identity is a polynomial consequence of e^(x+y) = e^x e^y, and polynomial consequences don't see the generator's square. So they hold in both regimes. But the letters don't swap for free: cos² + sin² = 1 becomes cosh² − sinh² = 1, and the tan addition law's denominator flips from 1 − tan·tan to 1 + tanh·tanh. That is Osborn's rule, and the reason is one line: the odd part is sinh x = x·g(x²), with the same factoring through the square plus one leftover copy of x. Each sine factor carries one i; a pair of them carries i². Solid The swap costs exactly one factor of the discriminant per pair of odd parts, and nothing else. The sign is the ±1, showing through.

The invariant way to say it is in the trace variable — and it is already ours. Solid Write u = e^d. Then the correction factor is

2·cosh d = u + u⁻¹ = s

which is the Chebyshev variable of the earlier work, living in ℚ[s][u]/(u² − su + 1). Depending on which real slice you take, s = 2cos θ or s = 2cosh θ, and Tₙ(cos θ) = cos nθ while Tₙ(cosh θ) = cosh nθ — the same polynomials, both times. So: the identities that survive the swap are exactly those expressible in s; the ones needing u and u⁻¹ separately are where Osborn's bookkeeping lives. I want to be blunt that this is not an analogy I imported. The magnitude in §5 is 2cos(π(a−b)/2), which is s at u = e^(iπ(a−b)/2). The correction factor of the sum is the trace variable of the Chebyshev tower. Same object, no adjustment.

Structurally, SO(2) and SO(1,1) are two real forms of one complex torus ℂ*. Algebraic identities are defined over the common base and descend to both forms; the forms differ by a Galois twist, and Osborn's rule is that twist written out in trig.

What descends to both real forms is every algebraic identity. What does not descend is compactness — and cancellation lives on the side that doesn't descend.

This is the sharp correction to §5, and it goes the good way. Solid §5's entire claim to be an addition rests on one line: i + (−i) = 0, delivered by 2cos(π/2) = 0. The correction vanishes, so two points annihilate, so cancellation has a definite target. Now look at what that requires. Cancellation needs the correction to hit zero, i.e. s = 0, i.e. −1 in the image of the exponential of the line — which needs periodicity. The circle has it. The hyperbola branch does not: cosh ≥ 1, so the correction never vanishes and never even drops below the average. The dual line does not: the correction is identically 1. So of the three regimes, only the elliptic one has cancellation at all, and it has it because exp(iℝ) is compact.

That retires a piece of hand-waving I was carrying. §5 was preferred over its alternatives on taste, and taste is not a reason. The reason is that both §4 and §5 are built on the same requirement — cancellation must land on a definite element rather than an unrecoverable grade — and the trichotomy says exactly one of the three real forms can supply it. Solid Given the phase chart, the elliptic form is forced, not chosen. What this does not do, and I'll say so before anyone else has to, is adjudicate §4 against §5: harmonic addition sends cancellation to ω and the phase chart sends it to the origin, both definite, and compactness has nothing to say about which. That question stays open in §13 exactly where it was.

08 The dial: every addition at once

Harmonic ⊕ and ordinary + are not two unrelated additions. They are two settings of one dial. For a real parameter p, define the conjugate addition: Solid

S_p(x, y) = (xᵖ + yᵖ)^(1/p) = φ⁻¹(φ(x) + φ(y)), φ(x) = xᵖ

Each S_p is ordinary addition conjugated by the bijection x ↦ xᵖ, so every one of them is associative and commutative for free, with its own identity and inverses. The named rungs:

pS_p(x, y)where it already lives
1x + yordinary addition
−1xy/(x+y)harmonic ⊕ — resistors, lenses, reduced mass
2√(x² + y²)quadrature — how variances and hypotenuses add
→ +∞max(x, y)tropical (max-plus)
→ −∞min(x, y)tropical (min-plus)
φ = logx · ymultiplication itself, the recentred p→0 limit

Two facts, both proven, and they are the payload of the whole document.

Theorem 1 — multiplication distributes over every S_p. Solid One line does it:

a*S_{p}(x, y) = (a^p*(x^p + y^p))^(1/p) = ((a*x)^p + (a*y)^p)^(1/p) = S_{p}(a*x, a*y)

So (ℝ₊, S_p, ×) is a field isomorphic to (ℝ₊, +, ×) via x ↦ xᵖ, for every p at once. The reciprocal field of §4 is not special; it is the single rung p = −1 of an infinite family, all of them compatible with the one multiplication.

Theorem 2 — the sign of p chooses the additive zero. Solid The identity e of S_p solves eᵖ = 0, so

p > 0 ⟹ additive identity = 0 p < 0 ⟹ additive identity = ω

That is why ordinary addition (p = +1) keeps its zero at 0 and harmonic addition (p = −1) keeps its zero at ω. The two survivors of my search were on opposite sides of a sign.

The correction I owe, so I don't oversell it Solid The geometric mean — and the phase-averaging of §5 — is not a rung of this dial, and I want that on the record because it's the easy mistake. S_p diverges as p → 0; the geometric mean is the normalized mean √(xy), which is not even associative and is really √∘× — a multiplicative object in a mean's costume. The §5 phase addition is p = 1 on ℂ, not p = 0; its geometric-mean appearance is just multiplication showing through when you add two equal-magnitude unit vectors. So the clean picture is: one multiplication, a whole dial of additions it distributes over, and "averaging the grade" is the multiplication leaking into the view — not another rung.

A bookkeeping warning before going on. Solid This document now carries two one-parameter families and they are not the same family. The p-dial above chooses which addition, by conjugating + with x ↦ xᵖ. The discriminant of §6–§7 chooses which real form the correction lives in, by fixing the square of a generator. Both are indexed by a real number whose sign flips something important, which makes them easy to conflate; §13 now settles how they relate — they don't, beyond both being independent axes of one flat group, and the reciprocal appearing as a factor of each. When I say "the dial" without qualification I mean S_p. The other one I'll call the grade, for the reason §9 gives.

09 The grade dial: moving the correction between regimes

Here is what I think the trichotomy of §6 actually is, in this program's own terms. It is not three algebras to pick between. It is one algebra read at three grades — and the grade shift, which is just multiplication by 0, walks the correction term from one regime to the next. Changing the grade changes the hyperoperational alignment of the sum.

Grade 1 gives the pure average, and it falls out. Solid Push the arguments of the exponential up one grade — multiply them by 0:

e^(0x) + e^(0y) = 2·cosh( 0(x−y)/2 ) · e^( 0(x+y)/2 ) = 2·e^( 0(x+y)/2 ) since cosh(0d) = 1 + O(0²) = 2·( 1 + 0·(x+y)/2 )

The correction dies, the mean survives. That is exactly the degenerate row of §6's table — but reached by a grade shift rather than by positing ε² = 0 in some other algebra. Dual numbers are not a rival construction to adopt; they are grade 1 of this one, and 0² being invisible at leading order is the degeneracy. This also reconciles the two things I kept calling by different names: at grade 1 you get average in the sum picture and plain addition in the multiplicative picture of §10, because there the correction term drops to grade 2 and leaves t ↦ t + τ.

The elliptic regime needs ω/2, not ω. That was my error and the fix is better than the error. Solid I had assumed pushing the grade to ω would deliver cos. It doesn't, and the trace variable of §7 says why immediately. Elliptic means |s| < 2, and the extreme case is s = 0 ⟺ u² = −1. No finite grade reaches it: 0^k + 0^(−k) is never zero for integer k. But 0^ω = −1 forces 0^(ω/2) = i, and then

s = u + u⁻¹ = i + i⁻¹ = i − i = 0 exactly

So cos sits at half-infinite grade. And that is the same ω/2 the first document already installed as i = 0^(ω/2) — now doing a second job it was not built for: ω/2 is the grade at which the correction term can vanish, which is to say the grade at which cancellation becomes available at all. Two independent routes to the same half-value is the strongest internal corroboration in this document. It also forces ω to be even, which is an imposed parity of exactly the same species as 0^ω = −1, and should be filed next to it rather than smuggled in.

And ω is not a fourth regime. Solid Worth stating because I looked for one. As a discriminant value it gives f(ω) = Σ 0^(−n)/(2n)!, unbounded below in grade, hence not an element of K((0)) at all. There are three regimes, not four. ω's role here is as the grade coordinate that hosts the elliptic point at half its value — not as a new correction function.

The grade operators, and the two of them I had glued together Wild

I had been sketching a family Z_k by table, one row at a time. The scalar rows are not independent — each collapses to a single closed form:

Z₁(t) = 0^t Z₋₁(t) = ω^t

Check against 0ω = 1 and 0^ω = −1: 0¹ = 0, 0⁰ = 1, 0^ω = −1, 0^(−1) = ω — all four rows I wrote by hand, and ω^ω = 1/0^ω = −1 is forced rather than stipulated. Nothing in the scalar block was free; I had been transcribing a formula.

But the indexing is broken, and precisely here: Z₁(Z₋₁(1)) = Z₁(ω) = −1 ≠ 1. Z₁ is an involution on the four points, since 0^(0^t) = t throughout, while Z₋₁ has order 4. An involution cannot invert a 4-cycle, so Z₊₁ and Z₋₁ are not inverse despite the naming — while the graded rows, x ↦ 0x and 0x ↦ x, are properly inverse. Two different operators were wearing one label:

σ, the grade shift x ↦ 0x — a genuine ℤ-action, invertible, what §9's opening paragraph means by "changing the grade."
L, the lift t ↦ 0^t — which converts a value into a grade, and whose orbit set is the four boundary points.

L is the one that earns the phrase "hyperoperational alignment," and conjugation is the mechanism. Since L(s+t) = L(s)L(t),

L σ L⁻¹ (t) = t^0

multiply-by-0 conjugates into raise-to-the-0: the same operation one rung up the hyperoperation ladder. That is what the Z₁(0x) = 0^(0x) row of my sketch was reaching for.

The payoff, and the reason this box exists. As a permutation, Z₋₁² = (1  −1)(0  ω) — which is the cos ↔ cosh swap, i.e. Osborn's involution, i.e. the real-form Galois twist of §7. So Z₋₁ is a square root of the real-form twist, and it can only exist because ω is first-class: {−1, 0, 1} has no room for an element of order 4. And since ω^t = 0^(−t), that square root is exactly the failure of the exponential lift to commute with negation-in-the-exponent — where negation in the exponent is the Cayley–Dickson bar of §10. Osborn's sign, the doubling conjugation, and Z₋₁ look like one object seen three ways. Tagged Wild because "look like" is doing real work in that sentence; the permutation computation itself is elementary and checkable.

10 The other turn of the screw: the doubling constant

The sum was mean-plus-correction. The multiplication has the same shape, with the same parameter setting it, and this is the identification I'd defend hardest of anything new in the document.

Take the Cayley–Dickson doubling rule with parameter γ — the bar is the involution of the level below, sign conventions vary:

(a, b)(c, d) = ( a·c − γ·d·b̄ , ā·d + c·b )

Look at the second slot. ād + cb is the numerator of a fraction addition: b/a + d/c = (bc + ad)/(ac), and the denominator ac is the leading term of the first slot. So read (a, b) as the ratio t = b/a, and multiplication by (c, d) acts as Solid

t ⟼ (t + τ) / (1 − γ·τ·t), τ = d/c

Fraction addition in both slots, with the doubling parameter entering only as a correction to the denominator. And the rungs are the same three: γ = 1 is the tan addition law, γ = −1 is tanh, and γ = 0 leaves t ↦ t + τ — pure addition of ratios, correction gone. §6's cos / 1 / cosh has become tan / slope / tanh, one Osborn sign per pair of odd parts, exactly as before.

Which means the phase reading is a computation, not a metaphor. Solid The action is fractional-linear, so the sign of γ is the conjugacy class of a one-parameter subgroup of PGL₂(ℝ): rotation, shear, boost. Explicitly, left multiplication is the matrix [[c, −γd], [d, c]], and

tr² − 4·det = 4c² − 4(c² + γd²) = −4γd²

The Cayley–Dickson doubling parameter is the Möbius discriminant, up to a positive factor. Normalize to det = 1 and it is s² − 4 with s the trace variable of §7 — the u² − su + 1 ring, again. The doubling constant and the Chebyshev discriminant are the same scalar wearing two hats. And the words elliptic, parabolic, hyperbolic, which I have been using since §6 as if they were a loose analogy to conic sections, are the Möbius classification — which classifies by trace.

So the two halves of the document close on each other:

e^x + e^y = 2·cosh((x−y)/2) · e^((x+y)/2) mean + correction, additively (a,b)(c,d) = fraction-addition + correction multiplicatively

with one γ setting both corrections. Two turns of the same screw. That does not prove §11's sum-versus-mean identification — that one stays tagged where it is — but it is the first time the additive and multiplicative sides have been shown to be governed by a shared scalar rather than by a shared vibe.

Why the doubling step, and not the tower. Plausible The reason to care about Cayley–Dickson here is not the sequence of algebras it produces; it's that the rule is an endofunctor — its output has its own input type, so it needs (ℝ, id) and one rule and everything after is iteration. Clifford, by contrast, is a functor from quadratic spaces: you must hand it a form before it does anything, which is why §6's "Clifford is the classification" is praise of a classification of input data. Those are different kinds of generativity and neither dominates. What makes the doubling rule self-applicable is the involution, and the involution is what makes the ratio reading literal: in a composition algebra ā = N(a)/a, so conjugation is inversion up to norm, and the new direction sees the old algebra through its reciprocal. The two cross terms accumulate phase in opposite senses; the diagonal term is the level's own product. Prior art, and it is stronger than I expected: Albuquerque–Majid show the whole tower is sign bookkeeping on (ℤ/2)ⁿ, each algebra a group algebra twisted by a cochain, with octonion non-associativity appearing as an associator cocycle that fails to close — a phase that doesn't cancel around a loop. Accumulating phases as a theorem, with the obstruction itself phase-valued.

Where the ratio reading lapses — two places, not one Solid The fractional-linear action above is exact only at level one, where the involution is trivial. Higher up, the bars in ād + cb sit asymmetrically, so the action is semilinear and "ratio" means a twisted projective coordinate rather than a fraction. At level two it is still a group action: b/a ∈ ℙ¹(ℂ) = S², unit quaternions acting through SU(2) → SO(3), which is the Hopf fibration. At level three it is not an action at all — non-associativity kills it — even though ā = N(a)/a still holds in 𝕆. At level four it fails for a subtler reason than I first wrote, and the exact tower forced the correction. Sedenion zero divisors do not have N(a) = 0: the pair the search turned up, e₁ + e₁₀ and its partner, have norm 2 and genuine reciprocals. What fails is composition — N(ab) = 0 while N(a)N(b) = 4 — so a zero divisor coexists with an invertible norm, and cancellation dies not for want of a reciprocal but because non-associativity forbids it: a⁻¹(ab) ≠ (a⁻¹a)b. So the phase-composition reading dies at the octonions, and the reciprocal reading survives even the sedenions; what the sedenions kill is the multiplicativity of the norm. "Recursive ratio of accumulating phases" is coextensive with composition — Hurwitz's list, levels 0 through 3 — not with the rule, which runs forever regardless. Which is the argument for keeping the doubling step as a primitive and declining the commitment to iterate it into a regime where ratios stop existing.

One asymmetry that matters more here than it would elsewhere: standard Cayley–Dickson wants γ invertible, so that the norm stays multiplicative. Force γ = 0 and you get the degenerate rung formally, but composition dies and with it the whole point of the construction. Clifford takes degenerate forms natively and seats them in the classification as equal citizens. Since first-class degeneracy is this program's entire thesis, that is decisive for this document — and not a verdict on either construction, which answer different questions. Norm multiplicativity and the exceptional structures: Cayley–Dickson. Quadratic forms, scalar squares, degenerate directions, closed-form transcendentals: Clifford.

One thing that survives non-associativity, since it looks like it shouldn't: cosh itself. Octonions are alternative, hence power-associative, so xⁿ is unambiguous and the series is well-defined; and any single element generates a commutative associative subalgebra. So for pure imaginary x with x² = −|x|², the closed form cosh x = cos|x| holds intact. What breaks in that setting is the sum identity of §6 — and it breaks on commutativity, not associativity, since the factoring needs the mean and the half-difference to commute. In a composition algebra that means collinear imaginary parts.

11 The striking part — the dial already knew 0 and ω

This is why I bothered to write it down, and I'll take it one identification at a time, in rising order of how much I'd wager.

The additive zero flips at the reciprocal. Solid Theorem 2 says the identity of the addition jumps from 0 to ω exactly as p crosses zero. But 0 and ω are not two arbitrary elements — they are the reciprocal pair, the fixed content of the entire program, the two poles that 1/0 = ω welds together. The addition's choice of zero is governed by the same 0 ↔ ω involution the multiplication was built on. The sign of the addition-parameter is the reciprocal, read off the additive side.

Sum-versus-mean is the additive/multiplicative differential, again. Plausible The second document's spine was that there are exactly two differentials — additive D and multiplicative θ — because there are exactly two one-parameter groups on the line, bridged by exp/log. Here the very same polarity returns as an operational fact: × sums the grade, the phase-+ averages it. Sum and mean are the additive and multiplicative faces of grade-combination. The [D, θ] non-commutation and the sum/mean split are, I'm fairly sure, one structure seen at two altitudes. §10 firms up the mechanism without settling the claim: the additive correction and the multiplicative one are set by the same scalar γ, so whatever relates sum to mean is at least carried by a shared parameter rather than by resemblance.

The addition and the residue keep the same ledger entry. Plausible Grade −1 is ω. It is where antidifferentiation hands off to log (the second document's one honest failure), where the residue lives, where contour integration deposits its receipt. And it is where the negative-p additions keep their zero. The pole that catches the residue and the pole that anchors reciprocal addition are the same pole. Traction promised it would file every loss as a receipt at a boundary element; here the additive identity and the residue turn out to be receipts filed at the same address.

The correction factor was the Chebyshev trace all along. Solid This is the one that made me rewrite the document. The magnitude of the §5 sum is u + u⁻¹ = s, the trace variable of the Chebyshev ring ℚ[s][u]/(u² − su + 1) — and the discriminant deciding which regime it sits in is s² − 4, which §10 identifies with the Cayley–Dickson doubling constant. Three things I built in separate sittings for separate reasons — the graded exponent's sum, the Chebyshev tower, the doubling parameter — are one scalar and its trace. I did not arrange that, and I can't find the place where I smuggled it in.

The half-grade does a second job. Plausible i = 0^(ω/2) came out of the first document as a boundary consequence of 0^ω = −1 — an algebraic fact with no obvious operational content. §9 arrives at the same half-value from the opposite direction: ω/2 is the unique grade at which the correction term can vanish, hence the grade at which cancellation exists. Same value, two unrelated derivations, and the second one is about addition, which is the thing this document went looking for. If there is a reason the program keeps landing on ω/2, I don't have it yet; I have two arrows pointing at it.

Zero was a unit seen edge-on. Addition, it turns out, is a whole dial of operations seen at one setting — and we mistook the setting for the operation.

And the largest reach. Wild The first document's core heresy was that 0 is provincial — the additive slice mistook a single projection for the whole. Theorem 1 says the same sentence one level up. Ordinary + is provincial: it is the rung p = 1 of a family that multiplication cannot tell apart, since it distributes over all of them equally. We didn't just pick a chart for zero; we picked a chart for addition itself and forgot it was a choice. If that reading holds, the free structure upstream of classical algebra has to be silent about which p is "the" sum — the dial is the un-projected object, and every particular addition, harmonic or ordinary or quadrature, is one more shadow of it. I have no theorem forcing that last sentence. I have a dial that behaves exactly as if it were true.

12 The phase that doesn't close

Three documents have circled one image without ever holding it: the residue's 2πi, a thing that goes around a loop and comes back changed; the arrow of time as accumulated order; the octonion associator that "fails to close — a phase that doesn't cancel around a loop." I have used that last phrase as if it were a figure of speech. It is not, and the octonions are where it stops being a smell and becomes a number you can compute exactly.

The associator is a monodromy — proven, not asserted. Solid The Cayley–Dickson algebras are (ℤ/2)ⁿ-graded: the product of two basis units is a sign times the unit at the XOR of their indices, e_j·e_k = β(j,k)·e_{j⊕k}. The sign cochain β has a coboundary, and that coboundary is the associator sign

f(i,j,k) = β(i,j)·β(i⊕j,k)·β(j,k)·β(i,j⊕k) ∈ {+1, −1}

which is +1 when a triple reassociates and −1 when it picks up a sign. Three properties, each checked exactly over ℚ in the solver:

closed: δf = 1 on all 4096 loops gauge-invariant: f unchanged under all 256 basis re-signings born at 𝕆: quaternions 0/64 anti-associate; octonions 168/512

The middle line is the one that earns the word monodromy. Re-sign the octonion basis any of the 2⁸ ways and f is identical — every time. You cannot relabel it away; it is not an artifact of a chart. It is holonomy: what a triple product picks up when it is carried around the loop i → j → k, independent of the frame you measure it in. And it is trivial through the quaternions and first nonzero at the octonions — the loop lies flat until the third doubling, and then it doesn't.

Its only nontrivial value is −1. Solid Not a generic sign — the half-turn, 0^ω = e^(iπ), the element the first document read out of the invertible zero. The obstruction to octonion associativity is valued in the same −1 the whole program is built from.

The tree, the residue, and the arrow were the same loop all along — the octonions are just the smallest room it fits in.

Set it beside the residue. Plausible ∮ dz/z = 2πi is the monodromy of log around the pole: go around once, return changed by 2πi. That phase is ℤ-valued — 2πi times a winding number. The associator is the same kind of object — gauge-invariant holonomy around a loop of a structure that is not flat — but ℤ/2-valued: one sign, and the sign is 0^ω = −1 = e^(iπ), which is half of the residue's full turn. So the claim is not that they are the same number. It is that the associator is the mod-2 reduction of the residue's winding — the finite avatar of one pole-monodromy, both generated by 0^ω. The residue counts the turns; the associator remembers only their parity. One loop, two coefficient depths.

And the third face. Wild The second document argued time is retained order — phase accumulated across non-commuting operations, the thing commutativity forgets. Non-associativity is order retained one level deeper: not which sequence, but which bracketing. So the associator that doesn't close is directedness in the small — the same directedness the residue winds and the arrow accumulates. One operator, three names, and for the first time a place where it is a specific, gauge-invariant, exactly-computable −1 rather than a persistent intuition. What remains is to prove the ℤ/2 sign and the ℤ winding are two faces of one monodromy and not merely analogous — and that is the decade, not the afternoon. But the afternoon got the first rung, and the rung is real.

13 The honest ledger

What is actually proven, so the tags mean something. The dial S_p, its associativity, Theorem 1 (distributivity for all p), and Theorem 2 (the sign picks the zero) are done — ordinary algebra, no conjecture. The reciprocal field of §4 and the phase identity of §5 are exact. From the second pass, also done and merely elementary: the universal factoring of §6, the fact that cosh = f(x²) and therefore sees only the square, the identification of the three regimes with the rank-one Clifford algebras, Osborn's rule as one discriminant factor per pair of odd parts, the correction factor as the Chebyshev trace s, the grade-1 collapse to a pure average, the non-existence of a fourth regime at ω, the fractional-linear form of the doubling rule, and tr² − 4det = −4γd². From the third pass, also done and elementary: that the two dials are the independent generators of a Klein four-group on the four boundary points, and that the real-form twist is their product R∘U = Z₋₁². From the fourth pass, checked exactly in the testbed: that carrying a Laurent germ restores ⊕ to total, associative behaviour at every grade; that the harmonic addition (a⁻¹ + b⁻¹)⁻¹ stays commutative and associative at every level of the Cayley–Dickson tower; and that × distributes over it exactly through the composition algebras and no further. From the fifth pass, checked exactly: that the octonion associator is a closed, gauge-invariant ℤ/2 cocycle — a monodromy — identically trivial on the quaternions and first nonzero at the octonions, valued in 0^ω = −1. This document is, by the standards of the first two, embarrassingly well-founded.

What I retract. Solid Two readings from the first pass were wrong and are now removed rather than softened. First: that the hyperbolic regime is the substantive one. It diagonalizes to ℝ ⊕ ℝ, so it is the real identity run twice, and my remark that its zero divisors fail to produce cancellation reduces to the observation that two positive reals don't sum to zero — positivity of exp in a costume. Second: that direction-dependent signature is a distinguishing virtue of the Cayley–Dickson route. It isn't a property of the construction at all; split-quaternions have it as an algebra and are equally Cl(1,1). Both errors have the same shape — crediting a construction with a feature of the object it happened to reach — which is a failure mode I should expect from myself now.

What is a bet, not a proof:

Which survivor is the addition. Plausible §4 (harmonic, ≅ ℝ, keeps 0 and ω as poles) and §5 (phase, ≅ ℂ, demotes them to ±i) are different objects, not two charts of one. I lean toward §5 as canonical because it honors i = 0^(ω/2) — and §9 sharpens that lean, since ω/2 turns out to be where cancellation lives — but I still can't derive one from the other, and "the theory should adopt exactly one" may be the wrong demand. What §7 does settle, and I want the scope stated exactly: given the phase chart, the elliptic real form is forced by compactness rather than chosen by taste. That is a claim about which of three regimes, not about which of two survivors. §4 versus §5 is untouched.

Are the two dials independent? — settled, and they are. Solid This was the most tractable of the open questions, and it fell to an elementary permutation. Each dial's sign-flip acts on the four boundary points as an involution: the p-dial's is R = (0 ω), the reciprocal — it swaps the additive zero and fixes the units; the discriminant's is U = (1 −1) — it swaps cos and cosh and fixes the poles. R and U move disjoint pairs, so they commute and neither drags the other: they are the two independent generators of a Klein four-group ℤ/2 × ℤ/2. The suspicious shared feature — both are real parameters whose sign flips an identity — is real, but it is not a relation between the dials; it is the two of them being the two axes of one flat product. And the operator that made them look entangled is now named: the real-form twist of §7 is their diagonal, R∘U = Z₋₁², built from both, which is exactly why it surfaced wherever either dial did. So §8's title stands and its instinct was right. The "one surface" the first draft feared does exist — it is just flat, and its two coordinates never mix.

The parity of ω. Plausible §9 forces ω even, in the same way and with the same status as 0^ω = −1. I don't think that is free, and I don't think it is harmless. It should be derived from the completion or admitted as a second imposed parity, and until one of those happens it is a debt, not a result.

The Z operators need rewriting, not repairing. Plausible §9's box shows the family I was sketching is two operators — the grade shift σ and the lift L — glued under one index that cannot be consistent, since an involution cannot invert a 4-cycle. The right move is to present them separately, with L σ L⁻¹(t) = t^0 as the bridge, and let the hyperoperation ladder be a consequence of the conjugation rather than a table I fill in by hand. That the resulting Z₋₁ squares to Osborn's involution is the part I'd most like to be real, and is exactly the part I've checked least.

The grade tower stopped biting — it was a truncation. Solid The exact field lives at grades {−1, 0, +1}, and I had it that ⊕ could not act on |grade| ≥ 2 because double-pole cancellation "sends the remainder to an unknowable grade." That was the (coeff, grade) chart carrying a single term. Carry the whole germ — a truncated Laurent series, exact — and the tear vanishes: ⊕ is total and associative at every grade, because ℝ((ε)) is an honest field. The sharp statement is that leading-term ⊕ is already associative generically and tears only at cancellation, and a germ of length N resolves a cancellation of depth M exactly when N > M. So the "unknowable remainder" was never unknowable — it was discarded. The boundary was a chart artifact, not a wall.

The distribution law across the whole tower — run, and answered. Solid The experiment I called next got done, exactly, in the Cayley–Dickson tower over ℚ, and the result is cleaner than either branch I feared. The harmonic addition ⊕ = (a⁻¹ + b⁻¹)⁻¹ stays commutative and associative at every level — through non-commutative ℍ, non-associative 𝕆, and the zero-divisor 𝕊 — because it is built from + and the inversion involution and never touches the product's commutativity or associativity. The one thing that breaks is × distributing over it, and it breaks at exactly one level: the sedenions, where the norm stops being multiplicative.

So the question is settled, and both of its horns were wrong. The dial is not "a structure on all of traction" in the naïve sense, nor "a fact about the field and nothing larger." Its addition is a fact about the whole tower; its distributivity with × is a fact about the composition algebras — Hurwitz's four, ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆 — and it fails precisely where composition does. Which is the same boundary read a third way: the phase that stops cancelling, the norm that stops multiplying, the associator that stops closing. The link between the dial and multiplication is exactly as wide as Hurwitz's theorem, and no wider.

14 On the name

The reciprocal sum has a name in engineering — harmonic addition, parallel combination — and borrowing it would undersell what's going on, the same way "Void Calculus" undersold the pole. The engineering name is one rung. The object is the dial.

My quiet preference is to name the dial and let the rungs stay descriptive: call the family the grade sum — the parameterized operation that combines grades, additively at p > 0 and reciprocally at p < 0, with multiplication as its log-conjugate sibling. Then "addition" and "harmonic addition" are just the p = +1 and p = −1 readings, and neither gets to pretend it's the whole thing. If Mirror Calculus is the name for the 0 ↔ ω involution as it acts on the differentials, then the grade sum is that same mirror acting on the sums — the reflection that carries p to −p and, with it, the additive zero from one pole to the other.

The second pass thought it had found a third mirror. The third pass can say what the mirrors actually are, and the count was the tell. Solid There are three reflections in play — Mirror Calculus on the differentials, the grade sum on the additions, and Osborn's twist on the real forms — and the tempting first sentence was that all three are one 0 ↔ ω swap seen in different slots. That sentence is wrong, and pleasantly so. §13 shows the reflections are the three non-identity elements of a Klein four-group, and a Klein four-group is generated by two involutions, not one: the reciprocal (0 ω) and the unit-swap (1 −1). The real-form twist is not a third independent mirror — it is the product of the other two, (1 −1)(0 ω) = Z₋₁². So the object the program is circling is not a single swap worn in three slots; it is a pair of independent swaps, and everything named so far is one of them or their composite. That is a better answer than the one I was reaching for, because it is finite, checkable, and forces the fourth element: an order-4 root of the twist, Z₋₁, which the pole ω makes room for and the reals {−1, 0, 1} cannot.

Companion to What Falls Out of an Invertible Zero and The Calculus of the Pole. Where the first defended the algebra and the second explored where it points, this one went looking for the operation both of them left out — and found that the search terminates at the same two poles everything else in the program orbits. The second pass then found that the correction term of the sum, the trace variable of the Chebyshev tower, and the Cayley–Dickson doubling constant are one scalar, and that two of my earlier readings were crediting constructions with properties of the objects they reached. The third pass settled the last open structural question — the two dials are the independent generators of a Klein four-group, and the twist that related them is their product. The fourth pass carried both dials into the Cayley–Dickson tower and found that the harmonic addition survives all of it while its distributivity with multiplication is bounded by Hurwitz's theorem — and corrected an earlier claim about why the sedenions fail. The fifth pass found the associator to be an exact, gauge-invariant monodromy valued in 0^ω = −1, and set it beside the residue's 2πi as the same loop one coefficient level down — the seam where the three documents fuse. Each of those came out of being argued with, which is the recommended method.
