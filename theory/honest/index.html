Core Commitments

Core commitments are the assumptions of a metatheory: They are subjective, nuanced, and definitional. Nobody is obligated to accept an assumption - they are always open for revisal. But, to accept an assumption means to carry the burden of its inevitable conclusions.

Honest Accounting

Honest logic asks: How many assumptions are being made? How many assumptions are being declared? What is the limiting factor for adding assumptions? When is it appropriate to redefine or remove an assumption? Are some assumptions better than others?

Conservation of information is the most reliable observation ever made by mankind. It's the cornerstone of physics, the constraint placed on every physical phenomenon. Erasing information sweeps the inconvenient truths under the rug. Honest logic doesn't do this.

Honesty means never over-claiming what is known. It also means fully qualifying what is known, and understanding degrees of confidence.

Totality: The tools always work, they never break down, they never fail to produce an answer. Reversibility: Data is never erased, it's traced.

Facts mean nothing without receipts. Receipts are an honest accounting of where you started, what happened along the way, and what got lost in the process. A receipt may not erase information, nor may it duplicate or synthesize information. Receipts are load-bearing: they are not just bookkeeping devices, they are the proofs.

Objectivity

If you're a first-hand witness, of sound mind and without ulterior motives, you could claim to possess an objective truth. Perhaps this is of great value to you, personally. But it cannot be transferred to any other person. If, however, you possess evidence that serves as a surrogate witness, then you have more than a personal anecdote: you have something that can be valuable to anyone.

It would be improper to insist someone accept your first-hand account, without evidence, as an objective truth from their perspective. And you'd be lying to yourself if you believed anything but a nonzero chance that your eyes have deceived you. With no other witness to confirm your accounting, you simply cannot be certain the receipt is perfectly constructed within your own head. Therefore, a first-hand witness does not possess an objective truth - they possess a subjective one, until the receipts can be found or reconstructed.

A truth can only be objective relative to a set of assumptions.

  1. Proven to be true
  2. Disproven to be false
  3. Proven to not result in an inconsistency.

All three conditions must be met for a truth to be considered "objective". This may seem like an unusually high standard, but it's the only honest standard to have. Some formal systems recognize LEM (Law of Excluded Middle), thus relieving themselves the burden of thorough verification. But more assumptions can't bring you closer to objectivity - because assumptions are subjective, contingent on acceptance.

An objective truth contains all its preconditions.

Subjectivity

A truth is subjective when any of the following are true:

  1. The truth is foundational, axiomatic, assumed.
  2. The truth relies on some other subjective truth.
  3. The basal assumptions are underdetermined.

All statements are subjective until proven otherwise.

If we're being honest, there probably is an objective truth, without precondition, behind every mystery of the cosmos. But that's irrelevant. We only know what we know, and what we know is very little.

Inconsistency

Assumptions are made voluntarily, but once accepted, all conclusions drawn from those assumptions are inherited. And any of those conclusions could be inconsistent, unless proven otherwise.

Inconsistencies are not necessarily fatal: they can be isolated, quarantined, and ignored. But that comes with a cost: every inconsistency requires the system to adopt more structure. Sometimes this is acceptable, especially when no other options exist. But when given the choice between different beliefs, choosing one for its proven consistency and sparse assumptions is not only imperative, it's practical.

Honest Logic proposes a system for qualifying assumptions and axioms, judging truth and provability, without violating basic epistemic standards.

Example: Peano Arithmetic

Counting things is not an abstract concept. Mankind has been counting things since the dawn of time. It's difficult to imagine an alternate reality where the concept of "counting" would be non-applicable. It can be physically demonstrated, its utility is broad, and life without counting would likely be short and painful.

These are the hallmarks of strong axiomatic foundations, of which Peano is one of the best examples. It's abstract only in its formalization, but the concept it represents is inarguably timeless, foundational, and genuinely indispensable.

By contrast, consider the Axiom of Infinity. You can't physically act out infinity, as you can with Peano. You can't draw a picture of what the infinity would look like if it was "actually real" - whatever that means. You can't even clarify what the substance of the infinity is, because it is defined as itself, without any further clarification. There's no consensus about whether infinity actually exists, and even if it did nobody could ever confirm it.

The Axiom of Infinity is, honestly, a bad axiom. That doesn't mean it isn't useful, nor should any proof be tossed away by its mention. Honesty doesn't impose restrictions - it merely demands receipts.

Judging Axiom Quality

Honest Logic proposes four axes of judgment for axioms: Parsimony, Empirical Fit, Provenance, and Net-Complexity Convenience.

Parsimony

Fewer axioms is better.

Empirical Fit

Correlation with observable phenomena.

Provenance

Derivation from subjective reasoning beats bare stipulation.

Net-Complexity Convenience

Axioms that remove restrictions and special cases are higher quality than those that only add capability.
